DEFS = {"zip": "Zip code", "hrr": "Hospital Referral Region (HRR)", "mkt": "Community-detection market"}
COLORS = {"zip": BLUE, "hrr": ORANGE, "mkt": AQUA}

def add_shares(data, market):
    """Hospital-level data with each hospital's share of discharges in its market-year."""
    d = data.dropna(subset=[market]).copy()
    d["mkt_discharges"] = d.groupby([market, "year"])["tot_discharges"].transform("sum")
    d["share"] = d["tot_discharges"] / d["mkt_discharges"]
    return d

def market_table(data, market):
    """One row per market-year: HHI (0-10,000 scale), largest share, hospital count, discharges."""
    d = add_shares(data, market)
    return (d.groupby([market, "year"])
             .agg(hhi=("share", lambda s: (s ** 2).sum() * 10_000),
                  max_share=("share", "max"),
                  share_sum=("share", "sum"),
                  n_hosp=("provider_number", "nunique"),
                  discharges=("tot_discharges", "sum"))
             .reset_index())

1(b) Why the definition matters¶

Answer. HHI is built from market shares, and a share only means something relative to the group of hospitals in the denominator. A narrow market has few hospitals in it, so each one has a large share and HHI is high. A wide market has many hospitals, so shares and HHI are low. The same hospitals can look like monopolists or like competitors depending on where the boundary is.

  • Zip code is too narrow. In 2017, 94% of zip-code markets contain a single hospital, so HHI is 10,000 almost by construction. Patients routinely cross zip-code lines for hospital care, so this overstates market power.
  • HRR is wide. HRRs are built around where patients are referred for major specialized surgery, and the median HRR has 5 hospitals. Two hospitals at opposite ends of an HRR may be far too distant to be substitutes for a routine delivery or an emergency, so this can understate market power.
  • Community-detection markets are drawn from actual patient flows and land in the middle (median of 3 hospitals per market).

There is no single correct definition because the right market is the set of hospitals that patients and insurers would actually switch between, and that set is different for different services, different patients, and different insurers building a network. Any fixed geographic boundary is an approximation: it treats hospitals just outside the line as no competition at all and hospitals inside it as equal competitors. The community-detection measure is the most defensible of the three because it is based on observed behavior, but the honest conclusion is that concentration should be reported under more than one definition. Here, even the most generous definition (HRR) gives an average HHI above 2,500, a level usually treated as highly concentrated.

The simple average counts each market once, whatever its size. Excluding one-hospital markets matters most for zip codes, where nearly every market is a single hospital, and least for HRRs, where only 3% are. Neither adjustment removes the conclusion that hospital markets are concentrated: the discharge-weighted community-detection HHI is still above 2,500, and the gentle upward trend after 1999 is still there.

# What a careless join looks like: forgetting `year` in the join key, so each hospital-year
# is divided by its market's discharges summed over ALL years instead of that year's total.
d = df.dropna(subset=["mkt"]).copy()
wrong_totals = d.groupby("mkt")["tot_discharges"].sum().rename("mkt_discharges").reset_index()
bad_join = d.merge(wrong_totals, on="mkt")                      # should be on ["mkt", "year"]
bad_join["share"] = bad_join["tot_discharges"] / bad_join["mkt_discharges"]
bad_mkt = (bad_join.groupby(["mkt", "year"])
           .agg(share_sum=("share", "sum"), hhi=("share", lambda s: (s ** 2).sum() * 10_000)).reset_index())

print("Careless join, share sums within market-year: "
      f"min {bad_mkt['share_sum'].min():.3f}, mean {bad_mkt['share_sum'].mean():.3f}, max {bad_mkt['share_sum'].max():.3f}")
print(f"Market-years passing the sum-to-1 check: {np.isclose(bad_mkt['share_sum'], 1).mean():.1%}")
print(f"Average HHI, correct join:  {markets['mkt']['hhi'].mean():,.0f}")
print(f"Average HHI, careless join: {bad_mkt['hhi'].mean():,.0f}")

Careless join, share sums within market-year: min 0.001, mean 0.055, max 1.000
Market-years passing the sum-to-1 check: 0.0%
Average HHI, correct join:  5,447
Average HHI, careless join: 26

Answer. The check passes for all three definitions: in every one of the 52,390 zip, 6,064 HRR, and 11,027 community-detection market-years, shares sum to exactly 1.

To see what the check protects against, I deliberately made a common mistake: joining market totals on the market identifier alone and leaving out the year. Each hospital-year is then divided by its market's discharges added up across all twenty years. Shares within a market-year sum to 0.055 on average instead of 1, no market-year passes the check, and the average HHI falls from 5,447 to 26. That would make hospital markets look almost perfectly competitive.

In general, a careless join breaks the link between the numerator and the denominator. If the denominator is too large (extra years or extra hospitals included), shares are too small, they sum to less than 1, and HHI is understated. Because shares are squared, the error is magnified. If rows are duplicated by the join, shares sum to more than 1 and HHI is overstated. If hospitals are matched to the wrong market's total, sums are off in both directions.

One limit of the check: it confirms that the arithmetic is consistent, not that the assignment is right. If a hospital is placed in the wrong market before the totals are computed, shares in that market still sum to 1, and the HHI is wrong anyway. So the check is necessary but does not replace looking at whether hospitals are in sensible markets.

The two measures agree here because concentration in these markets is mostly about one large hospital, which is also why maximum share is a reasonable quick summary but HHI is the more complete one.

Answer. The average price roughly doubles, from $5,101 in 1998 to $10,601 in 2017 (the median goes from $4,624 to $9,398). The rise is steady except for a flat stretch in 2011–2013.

The distribution is wide and skewed to the right. Across all hospital-years the median is $7,112, the middle half of hospitals lies between $4,811 and $10,186, and the standard deviation is $5,074. The range runs from $6 to $97,689. Neither end is believable as a price for a hospital stay: 348 hospital-years are below $1,000 and 276 are above $30,000. The spread also grows over time: the standard deviation doubles from $3,006 in 1998 to $6,293 in 2017, and the gap between the 10th and 90th percentile hospitals goes from about $5,500 to about $13,300.

What it captures. It is average revenue per non-Medicare discharge after discounts, so it is closer to what hospitals are actually paid than a list (chargemaster) price would be.

What it does not capture.

  • It is not a negotiated price for any particular service. It is a ratio built from accounting totals.
  • It is not adjusted for case mix. A hospital treating sicker patients or doing more complex procedures has a higher "price" even if it charges the same for the same service.
  • "Non-Medicare" combines private insurance, Medicaid, and self-pay patients, so differences in payer mix move the number.
  • It is built from cost-report data, which contain reporting errors. That is where the $6 and $97,689 values come from.
  • It is in nominal dollars.

What that means for the trend. Consumer prices rose by roughly 50% over this period, so a doubling in nominal terms is closer to a 40% real increase. Some of the remaining increase may reflect more intensive treatment per stay and shifts in payer mix rather than higher prices for the same care. Because of the outliers and the skew, the mean sits about $500 to $1,200 above the median in every year, and the median is the safer summary. The direction of the trend is credible; the exact size is not.

Answer. Limiting the comparison to markets with 10,000 to 50,000 discharges a year reverses the sign. Hospitals in monopoly or duopoly markets are now more expensive in every year, by $582 on average, compared with $561 cheaper in the full sample. The gap grows over time, from $414 in 1998–2007 to $751 in 2008–2017, and reaches $1,070 in 2017 ($10,623 against $9,553).

Market size is a confound because it affects both things being compared. Small markets can only support one or two hospitals because of fixed costs, so small size causes concentration. Small, rural markets also have lower prices for reasons unrelated to competition: lower wages and costs, less complex cases, and less specialty care. In the raw data the "concentrated" group is mostly small markets and the "competitive" group is mostly large ones, so the low-price effect of being small is credited to concentration and hides the market-power effect. Comparing markets of similar size removes most of that, and the positive relationship predicted by theory appears.

The restriction does not make the groups identical. Within the band, concentrated markets are still smaller on average (about 17,800 discharges against 33,000), and their hospitals are larger (200 beds against 142), since one or two hospitals are serving a whole mid-sized market. Larger hospitals tend to offer more complex services, which could push their measured price up. So the restricted comparison is better than the raw one but is still a correlation.

2(e) Is it causal?¶

Answer. No. The size-restricted comparison in part (d) is consistent with concentration raising prices, but it is not causal evidence.

Why not.

  • Remaining confounders. Concentrated and competitive markets still differ in case mix, payer mix, local wages and costs, hospital size, and quality. Any of these could produce a price difference without market power playing a role.
  • Market structure is not random. The number of hospitals in a market is an outcome of demand, costs, and past entry, exit, and merger decisions. Causation can also run the other way: high prices and margins attract entry, and a hospital that is better or lower-cost gains share and ends up in a concentrated market.
  • The price measure is noisy. As discussed in part (a), it is not adjusted for case mix and it mixes payers.
  • The answer already changed sign once. Going from a $561 discount to a $582 premium by controlling for one variable shows how sensitive the comparison is to what is held fixed.

What a causal claim would need. A change in concentration that is unrelated to the other things that drive price. The standard approach is to study mergers or closures: follow prices at the affected hospitals before and after the event, and compare them with similar hospitals in markets where nothing changed (a difference-in-differences design), keeping each hospital as its own comparison over time. That works best with mergers that happened for reasons unrelated to local market conditions, for example when two national chains combine. It would also need a better price measure, ideally negotiated payments from insurance claims for a fixed set of services, adjusted for case mix.

Conclusion. Once markets of similar size are compared, hospitals in concentrated markets charge about 5% to 11% more in 2013–2017. That matches what theory predicts and what merger studies generally find, so I read it as suggestive evidence of market power, but this data alone cannot establish that concentration is the cause.
